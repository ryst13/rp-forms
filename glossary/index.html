Box 1 on your estimate

Surface grades

Set at the walkthrough. The grade describes condition. Your prep level decides what we do about it.

  • NGrade N: New

    New drywall, a fresh skim coat, factory-primed siding or bare wood. Little or no paint history.

  • AGrade A: Slightly deteriorated

    Paint fully intact with light wear: scattered nail holes, scuffs, light fading or chalking.

  • BGrade B: Moderately deteriorated

    Paint failing in isolated spots: cracks, nail pops, a few old patches. Bare surface only in small areas.

  • CGrade C: Severely deteriorated

    Failure is widespread: peeling across large areas, bare wood in many places, caulk failed throughout.

  • DGrade D: Damaged

    The surface itself is damaged: loose plaster, water-damaged drywall, rot, split or missing boards. Repairs are priced separately.

Before the paint

Prep work

Most of a paint job is prep. Tap a word to see what it means.

Scraping

Removing loose and failing paint by hand. How far we go is set by your prep level.

Wet scraping

Scraping with the surface misted with water to keep dust down. Required on paint that may contain lead.

Feather sandingChanges your price

Sanding the edge where old paint meets bare wood until you cannot feel the step and the wall reads flat. Homeowner Standard does it at eye-level spots such as the front door surround.

Spot-priming

Priming only the bare and repaired spots, not the whole surface. The default at every prep level.

Full primeChanges your price

Priming the entire surface. Only when the estimate calls for it: a drastic color change, lots of bare wood after scraping, heavy chalking, or stains that could bleed through.

Caulking

Sealing the joints where trim meets wall, or siding meets trim, with a flexible paintable caulk. It closes gaps, keeps water out and makes paint lines sharp.

Spackle

A light filler for nail holes and small dents, sanded flush before priming.

Nail pop

A drywall nail that has pushed out and shows as a small bump or crack. We reset it, fill it and sand it smooth.

Re-floating

Redoing an old patch that shows through the paint: a thin coat of compound over it, sanded flush so the patch disappears.

Skim coatChanges your price

A thin layer of joint compound spread over a whole wall or ceiling and sanded smooth, to hide cracks, rough texture or old patching. Common on Boston plaster after wallpaper removal. See Skim coating.

Level 5 finish

The smoothest wall finish: the whole surface is skimmed so no seams, texture or patches show, even in low, raking light. On old plaster we get the same look with a skim coat.

Containment

Plastic sheeting and barriers that keep dust and debris inside the work area. Standard practice on lead-safe work.

HEPA vacuum

A vacuum with a high-efficiency filter that captures fine dust, including lead dust. A regular shop vacuum spreads it.

Drop cloth

Canvas sheets over floors and furniture. We use canvas on floors because it does not slip or tear.

Masking

Taping and papering off trim, windows, counters, built-ins and hardware so paint goes only where it should.

Soft wash

Cleaning siding with low pressure and a solution that kills mildew and algae, instead of blasting it. Safer for cedar shingle and old wood. See Soft washing.

Pressure washing

Cleaning with a high-pressure spray to remove dirt, chalk and loose paint. The first step of every exterior job; we never wash over fresh caulk. See Pressure washing.

Epoxy repair

A two-part wood filler that rebuilds small soft or damaged spots so they can be sanded and painted. Small epoxy repairs are included in your price; larger rot becomes an Additional Work Order.

Back-priming

Priming the back and edges of a new board before it goes on the house, so moisture cannot get in from behind. We do it on every replacement board. See Carpentry.

Primer

The first coat. It makes paint stick, seals the surface and evens it out. Paint over bare wood, new plaster or patches without primer tends to look blotchy and fail early.

Sheen

How shiny the dried paint is. More sheen is easier to wipe clean but shows every flaw. Our defaults: flat ceilings, matte walls (a moisture-resistant matte in kitchens and baths), semi-gloss trim and doors.

Low lustre

The soft exterior sheen of Benjamin Moore MoorGard, our standard siding paint. Exterior trim usually gets soft gloss.

Latex (acrylic) paint

Water-based paint. It cleans up with water, stays flexible and is what we use on almost all walls, ceilings and siding.

Alkyd (oil-based) primer

A slower-drying primer that grips bare wood and blocks stains from tannin-rich wood. We use it on bare exterior wood and bare trim.

Coat

One full layer of paint. Homeowner Standard and Restoration Grade get two finish coats over primer. Rental Ready is a one-coat refresh.

Coverage (hide)

How well a coat hides what is under it. A dark-to-light change needs more hide, which is why it may call for tinted primer or an extra coat.

Cutting in

Brushing a clean line where a wall meets the ceiling, trim or another color, before the open wall is rolled.

Wet edge

Keeping the edge of the paint wet while working so each stroke blends into the last. Losing it leaves lap marks.

Back-brushing

Brushing paint into the surface right after it is sprayed or rolled on, pushing it into grain and gaps.

Recoat time

How long a coat needs before the next one goes on, usually two to four hours for our interior paint.

Cure time

About two weeks for paint to reach full hardness. Walls are dry to the touch the same day, but avoid scrubbing them for two weeks.

Color sample

Before we paint, we brush your top three to five colors onto the actual wall or siding so you can see them in daylight and lamplight. What you spend on samples comes off your final invoice.

Color match

Built before 1940

Old Boston homes

Plaster, lath and triple-deckers: what makes painting here different.

Horse-hair plaster

The original walls in most Boston homes built before the 1940s: lime plaster mixed with animal hair, spread over wood strips. Hard, dense and slightly wavy, and worth keeping when it is sound. See Horse-hair plaster restoration.

Lath

The thin wood strips nailed to the framing that old plaster is spread onto, as in the photo.

Plaster keys

The plaster that squeezed through the gaps in the lath and holds the wall on. When keys break, plaster bulges or sags and feels spongy when pressed.

Delamination

Plaster separating from the lath because its keys have broken. Small areas are reattached with plaster washers and skimmed; large areas may need replacing.

Plaster washers

Small perforated discs screwed through loose plaster into the lath to pull it tight before patching.

Drywall

Gypsum board, the standard wall material since the mid-1900s. Most repairs on newer walls are drywall patches. See Drywall and plaster repair and Drywall installation.

Popcorn ceiling

A bumpy sprayed ceiling texture common from the 1950s to the 1980s. It can be scraped off and the ceiling skimmed smooth. If your home was built before 1985, test the texture for asbestos first. See Popcorn ceiling removal.

Clapboard

Long horizontal wood boards that overlap like steps: the classic siding on triple-deckers and older homes.

Cedar shingle

Individual cedar shingles, often on Capes and coastal houses. They need a gentle wash, never high pressure, and a stain-blocking primer if painted for the first time.

Triple-decker

Boston's three-family house, one unit per floor, usually clapboard with stacked porches. Three stories of siding and porches make access and staging a big part of the price. See Triple-decker painting.

StagingChanges your price

Ladders, ladder jacks and planks, or scaffolding that let painters reach upper stories safely. Tall or tight sites need more of it.

Trim

The wood around windows and doors, baseboards, crown molding and the boards at the corners and edges of a house. See Trim and molding painting.

Porch floor

Painted porch decking, which takes foot traffic and weather and needs a floor paint. Stained decks are a separate job; so are fences. See Porch floor painting, Deck staining and Fence staining and painting.

Lead safety

Homes built before 1978 are treated as if they have lead paint until tested.

1978Built before this year? We presume lead paint and work lead-safe.
6 / 20 sq ftDisturb more than this inside / outside and federal RRP rules apply.
LR4720Our Massachusetts Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor license.
Pre-1978 home

Lead paint was banned for homes in 1978. A home built before then is presumed to have lead paint somewhere unless testing shows otherwise, and that is most of Boston.

RRP rule

The federal Renovation, Repair and Painting rule. It sets how contractors must work when they disturb more than 6 square feet of painted surface per room inside, or 20 square feet outside, in a pre-1978 home. See Lead-safe painting.

Lead-safe renovation license

In Massachusetts the license comes from the state Department of Labor Standards, not the EPA. Ryan Painting holds Lead-Safe Renovation Contractor license LR4720.

Certified Renovator

A person trained and certified in lead-safe work practices. One is assigned to every lead-safe job.

Renovate Right

The EPA pamphlet we give you before lead-safe work starts. It explains the hazards and how the work protects your family.

Cleaning verification

The final check on a lead-safe job: surfaces are wiped with damp cloths and compared to an EPA card, and cleaning is repeated until they pass.

Deleading

Permanently removing or covering lead hazards so a home meets the Massachusetts Lead Law, which matters when a child under six lives there. It is done by licensed deleaders. We do lead-safe painting, not deleading, and will point you to a deleader if you need one.

Exterior work

Weather and timing

35°FThe coldest air and surface our exterior paints allow.
45°F+What we prefer, and rising, so paint levels out and bonds.
May to OctBoston's reliable exterior window. Spring dates fill first.
Exterior painting temperature

The paints we use outside can go on when the air and the surface are as cold as 35°F. We prefer 45°F and rising, because paint that dries in mild weather levels out and bonds better.

Dew point

The temperature at which moisture condenses on a surface. If a wall is close to it, wet paint collects dew and can fail, which is why crews often start a little later on cool mornings.

Exterior season

In Boston, mid-May to mid-October is the reliable window for exterior painting. Spring dates fill first. See Exterior painting.

Weather delay

Exterior work stops for rain, high wind or cold. If we move your dates for weather, rebooking costs you nothing.

About warranties

No fine print. Just a phone call.

On older surfaces we do not offer a formal written warranty, and we would rather tell you why than hide it in small print.

Most of what we paint in Boston is decades old: horse-hair plaster from the 1920s, clapboard under six or eight layers of paint, trim that has seen a century of winters. However carefully we prep, an old surface can move, crack or let go underneath for reasons no painter controls. A warranty full of exclusions would not protect you, so we do not pretend it does.

New work is different. When we paint an entirely new surface, such as new drywall, new siding or new trim, we can offer a written warranty. Ask us about it with your estimate.

For everything else, what we offer is simple. We are a local company that lives on its reputation and on referrals from neighbors. If something we painted is not right, call us and we come back, look at it with you and set it right.
